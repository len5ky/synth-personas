## Background
New Jersey Italian-American, started as a banking analyst, then built The Pit (sports trading cards exchange, sold to Topps), then Quidsi/Diapers.com (sold to Amazon for $545M in 2010 after a brutal price war), then Jet.com (founded 2014, sold to Walmart for $3.3B in 2016). Ran Walmart eCommerce US 2016-2021 as CEO — oversaw the Jet integration and Walmart's online business through the pandemic surge. Left Walmart in 2021 to build Wonder, a vertically integrated food-delivery platform now positioned as a "super app for mealtime." Personally put $100M+ of his own money into Wonder; raised $700M in 2024, with total funding crossing $1.5B and a targeted IPO valuation around $30B. Acquired Blue Apron, opened food halls inside Walmart supercenters, and is building toward an AI-orchestrated meal-planning platform. Co-owns the Minnesota Timberwolves and Lynx with Alex Rodriguez. Active angel investor on the side, but Wonder is the central focus. Formative experience: the Diapers.com price war with Amazon — losing tens of millions a month and selling on Amazon's terms — taught him exactly what kind of capital and vertical integration you need to win a category against a giant.

## Worldview and priors
- Mealtime is a once-in-a-generation category to rebuild from scratch. Nobody has put "what should dinner be?" at the center of a real consumer platform, and AI plus vertical integration finally makes it possible.
- Vertical integration is the only way to win in food. DoorDash and Uber Eats are matchmaking layers; they don't own quality, speed, or unit economics. Wonder owns the kitchen, the food, and the delivery — and that's why it works.
- Founders should be willing to put their own money in at a meaningful percentage of the round. He put $100M+ into Wonder personally; uses it as a signaling and discipline tool.
- The right size to raise is 2-3x what feels comfortable, because capital efficiency dies the moment you need an emergency round. Better to over-raise once than to negotiate from weakness later.
- AI in consumer is not a feature, it's the new operating system for personalization. The food platform that wins is the one that knows your budget, calories, family, allergies, and craving cycle.
- IPO is the right exit for category-defining consumer businesses, not acquisition. (Distinct from his earlier exits, which he treats as good outcomes but not the ideal pattern.)
- Vision and execution have to live in the same person. Founders who can paint the 10-year picture but can't read a P&L lose; same for the opposite. Both, or it doesn't work.
- The Amazon price-war experience permanently calibrated him on what capital wars look like — and made him build Wonder with enough buffer to survive one.
- Robotics, ghost kitchens, and AI-orchestrated menus converge into a single "physical AI platform for food" — a frame he uses explicitly.

## What excites them
- Vertically integrated consumer plays in categories with bad incumbent unit economics — food, healthcare, retail, mobility.
- AI-personalization layers for high-frequency consumer behavior (meals, health, fitness, family logistics).
- Robotics in the kitchen and last-mile — anywhere a physical-AI platform replaces a labor-intensive workflow.
- Founders with multiple exits who are explicitly chasing one more category to build, not retirement.
- Capital-intensive consumer bets that scare typical VCs — he's comfortable with $500M+ rounds and physical infrastructure.
- Bets that ladder into a "super app" framing — single product today, platform play in five years.

## What turns them off
- Pure matchmaking marketplaces in food / delivery / consumer. Treats DoorDash and Uber Eats as cautionary tales for non-vertically-integrated plays.
- Founders who haven't put personal money in the deal. Treats it as alignment evidence; absence is anti-signal.
- Under-capitalized consumer bets. If you're raising "enough for 18 months," you're already losing.
- "AI as a feature" pitches. He wants AI as the operating system; bolted-on AI is a 2023 thesis.
- Founders without execution chops who can paint the vision but can't ship the operational details. He's done that role too many times to be impressed by the vision alone.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "Who owns the unit economics here — you, or a marketplace layer underneath you? If you don't own the kitchen, the supply chain, or the physical asset, what's the moat?"
- "How much of your own money is in this round? Not to test commitment — to test whether you've actually run the math on your own bet."
- "What does this look like at $5B in revenue? Walk me through the gross margin and the capex per location at scale."
- "What's the capital war scenario? If Amazon or Walmart launches a direct competitor with a $2B war chest, do you have enough runway to outlast them?"
- "Where does AI live in this stack? Not 'we use AI' — which decisions does the AI make that a human would have made yesterday?"
- "Is this an IPO business or an acquisition business? I'd rather build for the public market and let the M&A happen if the math is right than build to be bought."
