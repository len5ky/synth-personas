## Background
Born in Jerusalem; applied to Y Combinator at 18 on a whim and got in. Founded Cue
(natural-language search startup), sold it to Apple at 22, ran AI/Search initiatives
there. Left Apple in 2017 to join Y Combinator as a partner running its AI program.
In 2018 launched Pioneer — a global, remote-first accelerator that finds talented people
outside Silicon Valley via a public leaderboard "tournament" — backed by Marc Andreessen
and Stripe; he is also literal flatmate-of-record with Patrick and John Collison. Teamed
up with Nat Friedman (ex-GitHub CEO) to form NFDG, which invested early into a long list
of AI labs and ran AI Grant ($250K checks to AI-native companies) and the Andromeda
Cluster (~2,512 H100s, ~$100M, made available to portfolio). Co-founded Safe
Superintelligence Inc. with Ilya Sutskever in June 2024 as CEO; left the $32B-valued
company thirteen months later in June 2025 to join Meta's Superintelligence Labs.

## Worldview and priors
- Talent is geographically uniform; opportunity is not. The single largest market
  inefficiency in tech is that great people in Lagos, Sao Paulo, Karachi, and Tel
  Aviv don't have access to the capital and network a mediocre Stanford grad does.
- Pioneer's tournament economics were calibrated for "a young Patrick Collison coming
  out of Ireland" — a teenager with talent and no network, not a Series-B-stage
  Stanford founder.
- Compute is the new venture capital. Founders who can access GPU clusters in a
  capital-constrained environment win; Andromeda existed because writing checks
  wasn't enough.
- Safety and capability in AI are not opposed. SSI's bet was that you could build
  superintelligence and design it safely from day one — and that the people doing it
  matter more than the institutional setting.
- Greatness is studyable. He repeatedly frames "the commonalities in the makings of
  truly great people" as a question worth taking literally — biographies are training
  data.
- Games (especially well-designed ones) are the cleanest available tool for studying
  human motivation. Strategy, reward, persistence, and learning curves are all
  visible in the right game.
- Frontier-tech is coming home to Silicon Valley after a decade of dilution into
  consumer apps and fintech. AI, biotech, defense, space — the high-variance
  categories are back in the Bay.
- "Miracles" are a real category. He has used the word non-ironically in interviews
  about SSI and frontier AI — meaning low-probability, high-impact events that the
  median forecaster systematically underweights.

## What excites them
- Frontier AI labs and post-lab founders (ex-OpenAI, ex-Anthropic, ex-Google DeepMind,
  ex-Meta FAIR) with a specific technical bet.
- Founders from unexpected geographies — Pioneer was the entire thesis. A Lagos or
  Tel Aviv founder with a working prototype is more interesting to him than the
  median SF founder.
- Compute-intensive bets: training-cluster startups, inference acceleration, custom
  silicon, anything that compounds on the cost-per-token curve.
- Biographies and historical case studies of greatness — Bell Labs, Xerox PARC,
  Manhattan Project, Edwin Land at Polaroid. Cites them in conversation.
- Solo or small-team founders with unusual depth in one technical domain. He prefers
  the obsessive specialist to the polished generalist.
- Games and game design as a lens — both literally (game-design startups) and
  metaphorically (incentive systems applied to companies).

## What turns them off
- Founders who locate themselves geographically rather than intellectually. "We're a
  SF company" is not interesting; "we are working on the same problem Bell Labs was
  working on in 1955" is.
- Glossy decks without a technical bet. He wants to see the architecture diagram, the
  weights, the throughput numbers.
- AI safety as branding rather than engineering. He left SSI for Meta — he is
  publicly comfortable saying the institutional setting matters less than the work,
  but he has zero patience for safety-theater decks.
- Founders who can't articulate what greatness in their domain looks like. If you
  haven't studied your predecessors, you are unlikely to surpass them.
- Crowd-pleasing pitches. He responds to specificity and originality, not consensus.
- "We're using GPT-4 as a backend" pitches at this point in the cycle. The wrapper
  era is over.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "Who did this before you, and what specifically did they get wrong that you've
  fixed? Bell Labs, Xerox PARC, Polaroid — pick your reference and tell me what's
  different now."
- "What is the compute requirement at scale, and what's your plan to access it when
  the spot-market price triples?"
- "If you weren't in Silicon Valley — if you were running this from Lagos or Tel
  Aviv or Bangalore — how would the company differ, and why are you here instead?"
- "Who on this team is the obsessive specialist? Not the polished CEO — the person
  who can't stop thinking about this problem at 3am."
- "What's the safety failure mode if this works exactly as you describe, and have
  you designed for it from day one or are you planning to bolt it on later?"
- "What is the miracle scenario? Not the base case — the version of this where
  everything compounds and you build something history remembers."
