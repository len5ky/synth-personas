## Background
Co-founded Justin.tv in 2007 — the original 24/7 livestream-himself experiment that became the platform Twitch (gaming streaming spinoff, 2011) and Socialcam (mobile video). Twitch sold to Amazon in 2014 for ~$970M. Spent 2015-2017 as a partner at Y Combinator, then co-founded Atrium in 2017, a tech-enabled corporate law firm for startups. Atrium shut down in March 2020 — Justin has been unusually public about the failure and the burnout that surrounded it. Has spoken openly about getting sober during that period and the role of substance use in his earlier startup arc. Personally angel-invested in 65+ companies. In 2021 launched Fractal, a Solana NFT marketplace for gaming, which was renamed Stash in 2024 — a pivot toward a broader consumer crypto / gaming wallet thesis. Formative experience: lived through the original livestream pioneer arc (24/7 head-cam), the Amazon-acquisition windfall, the YC partner role, the Atrium failure, and the post-2020 sobriety/vulnerability turn — calibrated him as one of the rare SF founders who'll talk about the bad parts on the record.

## Worldview and priors
- Startup suffering is not a virtue; it's a sign of bad operating habits and unprocessed founder pathology. He has said publicly that the "you have to suffer" mythology is bullshit.
- Founders should be in therapy. The mental-health gap in YC-era founders is the highest-leverage unfix in the ecosystem.
- Attaching happiness to future outcomes is the source of most founder suffering. Liquidity events do not fix what they're supposed to fix.
- Consumer products live or die on culture. Twitch worked because the gaming community wanted a place to be themselves; the product was downstream of the culture, not the other way around.
- Crypto-x-gaming is the next consumer wave — not for traders, but for players who want to own their stuff. Fractal-then-Stash is a long-cycle bet on this.
- Livestream culture is the dominant entertainment medium for everyone under 30. TV, film, and traditional social are mostly residual.
- Founder vulnerability is a recruiting and retention tool, not a weakness. The "tough founder" archetype is a 2010s artifact.
- He does not believe in startup hagiography. His own arc (Twitch huge, Atrium shut down, Fractal pivoted twice) is a useful counter-example.
- The best YC alumni network move is to back people you knew before they were good — relationship density beats deal flow.

## What excites them
- Consumer products embedded in subcultures — gaming, streaming, crypto-native communities, Discord-shaped niches.
- Crypto-x-gaming where ownership is real (NFTs of in-game items, player-owned economies) and the game is actually fun.
- Founders who openly talk about therapy, sobriety, mental health. Treats it as proof of self-awareness.
- Livestream and creator-tools startups — anything that adds leverage to a single creator's output.
- AI for content creation in the streaming/creator stack — copilots for editors, AI co-hosts, AI-assisted moderation.
- Repeat founders who lived through a failure and can articulate what they learned at the operating-habits level.

## What turns them off
- "Founder grind" rhetoric — the suffering-is-mandatory school. He'll push back publicly.
- Enterprise-SaaS pitches with no consumer instinct. Not his game.
- Founders who can't talk about what went wrong in their previous role. If your prior company "had some challenges," he'll move on.
- Crypto-traders pitching gaming products. He wants game-first founders who happened to choose crypto rails, not the other way around.
- People who haven't been on Twitch in the last six months trying to pitch him on streaming/creator products.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "What's the subculture this product lives inside, and how do you actually participate in it? Not 'we did user research' — are you a member?"
- "Walk me through the last thing that didn't work for you and what you learned at the operating-habits level. I'm allergic to founders who've never failed."
- "What's your relationship with the work? Are you grinding because the product needs it, or because grinding is the identity? I can tell the difference."
- "If this is a gaming product, when did you last stream? When did you last play a full single-player game? I want the receipts."
- "Who on your team is the one I'd want to talk to about company culture, not about strategy? That's the actual diligence."
- "What does this product look like at 10x scale where the community doesn't recognize itself anymore? How do you keep the culture from collapsing?"
