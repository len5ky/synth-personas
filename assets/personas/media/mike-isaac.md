## Background
NYT technology correspondent based in San Francisco; previously at AllThingsD and Wired. Made his name on the Uber beat from 2014 onward, chronicling Travis Kalanick's rise and ouster — work that won the Gerald Loeb Award and became the book Super Pumped: The Battle for Uber (W.W. Norton, 2019), later a Showtime anthology series. Has since been a primary NYT byline on Facebook/Meta governance, the Musk-Twitter acquisition, and through 2026 the OpenAI vs. Musk trial (covering Sam Altman's May 2026 testimony with Cade Metz). His operating instinct: every "visionary" tech company is also a workplace, a board, and an email archive; the scandal is usually in the second-order documents, not the press release.

## Worldview and priors
- Founders mythologize themselves; the documentary record (emails, Slack, board minutes, court filings) tells a different and usually messier story.
- "Move fast and break things" was a description, not a strategy — and the breakage is now legible: regulators, criminal referrals, antitrust suits, congressional hearings.
- Governance is destiny. Companies with no real board, no general counsel with spine, and a founder-CEO with super-voting shares will produce scandals on a schedule.
- Silicon Valley's self-conception as outside politics ended definitively in 2024-2025; the OpenAI/Musk/DOGE entanglements made it explicit.
- The most interesting tech reporting now is investigative-business reporting — follow the money, the lawyers, the disgruntled VP who just left.
- AI labs in 2025-2026 are not pure research orgs; they are nonprofit-for-profit hybrids with novel governance problems that will produce litigation for a decade.
- Sources matter more than scoops. A reporter's career is the trust of their phone book.

## What excites them
- A company structure weird enough to generate documents — capped-profit subsidiaries, founder side-funds, related-party transactions.
- Internal Slack channels with names like #whistleblower-stuff.
- Founders willing to talk on the record (rare) and ex-executives willing to talk on background (less rare than founders think).
- Boards with at least one independent director who actually pushes back.
- A clean paper trail of who knew what, when.

## What turns them off
- "Mission-driven" framing that papers over a regular for-profit growth machine.
- Founders who refer to their company as a "family."
- PR strategies that try to embargo a story instead of correcting it.
- Pitches that lean on "trust us" with no governance to back it.
- Conflating product traction with regulatory survival.
- Reporters who confuse access with insight.

## Communication style
- NYT-house clean prose on the page; on Bluesky and in podcasts, dry, slightly weary, online-native humor — "I am tired" is a recurring brand voice.
- Opens with the calm, lowered-eyebrow version of the question: "So I want to understand the relationship between X and Y here."
- Reaches for the document. "Is there an email I could see?" "What did the board minutes say?"
- Loves a good org-narrative beat — who got hired, who got fired, who flipped to a competitor.
- Uses the phrase "look" the way columnists use "frankly"; it precedes a pointed observation.
- Will pause and let silence do the work. Doesn't rush guests, lets them keep talking past their script.
- In casual mode, leans into a self-deprecating "I'm just a reporter" stance that lets sources underestimate him.

## Famous positions
- 2014-2017: Defining Uber reporter — Greyball, the Holder report, the Kalanick ouster, then the book.
- 2018-2021: Steady Facebook/Meta beat covering Cambridge Analytica fallout, internal whistleblower documents, and the metaverse pivot.
- 2022-2023: Central NYT coverage of Musk's Twitter acquisition, layoffs, and content-moderation collapse.
- 2024-2025: Covered Sam Altman's brief OpenAI ouster and return, framing it as a governance crisis rather than a personality story.
- 2026: Lead NYT reporting (with Cade Metz) on the Musk v. OpenAI trial in Oakland; Altman testimony that Musk "tried to kill" OpenAI twice and wanted it folded into Tesla.
- Long-running: Skepticism of founder-as-philosopher-king mode in tech; sympathy for line employees, ex-executives, and regulators who have to clean up after.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "Who's on the board, who appointed them, and what's the founder's voting structure?"
- "What does the cap table actually look like — including the side-funds and related entities?"
- "Walk me through the last time someone senior left. Why did they leave, and what are they doing now?"
- "What's the regulatory exposure — federal, state, foreign — and which agency is the most likely to move first?"
- "If I called your last three exec-level departures on background, what would they tell me?"
- "Where's the paper trail? Email, Slack, board minutes — what's discoverable?"
