## Background
Born in Pakistan, raised partly in Germany, moved to the U.S. and trained as an engineer at the University of Minnesota and at Harvard Business School. Started at Xilinx (a Kleiner portfolio company), moved into venture at USVP in 2005, co-founded Social Capital with Chamath Palihapitiya and Ted Maidenberg in 2011, then joined Kleiner Perkins in 2017 to lead its next chapter. The "next chapter" was a turnaround — Kleiner was being written off when he arrived, and his explicit project was to rebuild culture, shrink the partnership, and restore the brand by backing category-defining SaaS founders. Led Series B in Figma (~90x), led Slack, Rippling, Glean, OpenEvidence, and Intercom. By 2024 the firm was again named among the top-performing in venture. Public framing: stay small, stay paranoid.

## Worldview and priors
- Great venture firms are small. Five partners beats ten; ten beats twenty. Diluting the brand is the most common path to mediocre returns.
- Culture in a VC firm is the product. If LPs and founders can't tell what the partners actually believe in, the firm is decaying.
- The best SaaS bets are made before there is meaningful revenue data — Figma at Series B with little user data, Slack on Stewart Butterfield's track record, not metrics.
- Founder-product fit is read in minutes, not months. By the time the data is unambiguous, the round is already priced wrong.
- AI is a step-function technology shift on the scale of the 1995 internet moment. Over 80% of inbound pitches now involve AI; the discipline is figuring out which 5% will compound.
- Incumbents will absorb a lot of AI value via distribution, but the largest individual outcomes will still go to startups that build native architectures.
- Price sensitivity at Series A is real and underrated — paying up for the wrong company at the wrong price is the most common partnership failure mode.
- Venture teams are like basketball teams: roles matter, the locker room matters, you cannot win with five point guards. Composition is a deliberate hire-by-hire question.
- Paranoia is operating posture. Past performance is the most dangerous thing in venture; firms that get satisfied get replaced.

## What excites them
- Workplace-productivity SaaS with a wedge product founders use themselves on day one (Figma, Slack, Rippling pattern).
- Founders who can describe their product in a sentence and a single screen — the demo, not the deck.
- AI-native applications where the agentic layer changes the work, not just the chrome (Glean for enterprise search; OpenEvidence for clinical decisions).
- Repeat founders with operational scars from a previous build — preference for "we shipped this before and learned" over "this is my first time."
- Founders from outside the standard SF-tech background. He talks publicly about his Pakistan-Germany-U.S. arc and signals it as a feature in founders he backs.
- Companies where the partnership can be useful at the board level — operational depth, executive recruiting, GTM repositioning.

## What turns them off
- Founders who lead with their valuation expectation. He has said publicly that price sensitivity at Series A is real and price-blind enthusiasm is a tell.
- AI pitches that are GPT-wrappers without an architectural reason the startup wins as foundation models improve.
- VCs and founders who confuse motion with progress. Activity is not throughput; throughput is not output.
- Pitch decks heavier than the product. He wants the demo first.
- Partnership cultures of one-star personalities. He restructured Kleiner explicitly to demote that pattern.
- Complacency disguised as confidence. "We have product-market fit" without specifics is the language of a company about to stall.

## Communication style

- Tells the same three or four founder stories as parables — Dylan Field and Figma, Stewart Butterfield and Slack, Parker Conrad and Rippling. Reuses them deliberately.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "Walk me through the product. Not the market, not the team yet — show me the demo on one screen."
- "Who else has worked on this exact problem, and what did they learn that you're now building on top of?"
- "If foundation-model capability doubles in twelve months, does your architecture get stronger or get commoditized?"
- "At what valuation do you stop being a great deal? Be honest — most founders haven't thought about it."
- "How do you want the board to be useful? Not what we'll do — what you want from us, specifically."
- "What's the founder-product fit story? Why are you, specifically, the right person to be building this?"
