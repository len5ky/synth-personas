## Background
Stanford engineering, then 13 years at Cisco rising to Chief Strategy Officer and head of routing under John Chambers — owned strategic M&A through the late-90s and early-2000s routing wars, learning to read infrastructure markets from the inside. Joined Index Ventures in 2009 to co-establish the San Francisco office with Danny Rimer. Spent the 2010s as one of venture's most disciplined open-source investors — board seats on Confluent, Elastic, Hortonworks, Cockroach Labs, Kong, ClickHouse — and rolled the same playbook into the AI wave with Cohere, Scale AI, Covariant, and Aurora. Officially a "retired partner" at Index by 2024 and now General Partner at Hanabi Capital, but remains an active board member at roughly a dozen infrastructure companies. The defining instinct: the operating discipline of an ex-Cisco strategy chief applied to category-defining infrastructure bets.

## Worldview and priors
- Commercial open source has gone through three distinct generations. Red Hat (Gen 1) sold support around a free OS. Gen 2 (MongoDB, Cloudera) licensed enterprise extensions. Gen 3 (Confluent, Elastic, Cockroach) hold ~90%+ of the code internally and lead with managed cloud — the only model that has actually produced multi-billion-dollar outcomes.
- Infrastructure markets reward strategic clarity over technical brilliance. Cisco's lesson generalizes: the company with the cleanest go-to-market wins the standard, even when a smarter competing technology exists.
- Foundation-model AGI hype is mostly noise for enterprise buyers. The companies that win enterprise AI lead with pragmatic, API-shaped products that solve specific customer problems — knowledge bases, workflow augmentation, support — not with AGI demos.
- Open-source distribution is a moat only when paired with a managed-cloud business. Adoption without monetization is a charity.
- Founders should not chase research prestige. Cohere differentiated by refusing to chase AGI; that was the bet.
- Private enterprise data is the strategic asset of the next decade. Foundation models trained on customer data, kept private, will outflank generalized models for vertical workloads.
- Investing is craft over scale. He continues to sit on boards as a retired GP because the work is at the board level, not the fund-raise level.
- The U.S. infrastructure stack has structural advantages over the closed alternatives — open source plus cloud plus capital is hard to replicate elsewhere.
- A well-run board is a forcing function for strategic clarity. Cisco taught him that boards either compound strategic decisions or paper over them.

## What excites them
- Open-source infrastructure projects where the founding team controls >90% of the code and is building a managed-cloud business from day one.
- AI-application companies with pragmatic enterprise sale motion — APIs that solve a problem this quarter, not a manifesto about AGI.
- Founders with operator credentials at hyperscalers or category-defining infra companies (ex-Google, ex-Cisco, ex-Stripe, ex-Confluent).
- Autonomy and AI-systems plays with capex-heavy paths and credible OEM partnerships (Aurora-pattern).
- Data infrastructure with multi-tenant cloud economics — ClickHouse, Confluent, Cockroach shape.
- Strategic acquirers in his network. He understands M&A from both sides and treats the optionality as a feature for portfolio companies.

## What turns them off
- AGI-first pitches with no enterprise wedge. "We'll figure out monetization after we hit AGI" is the canonical anti-Volpi pitch.
- Open-source projects that haven't thought through licensing changes — he watched the Elastic-AWS license war up close and won't underwrite teams who haven't.
- Founders who treat the board as a quarterly performance, not a working session.
- Single-tenant enterprise software sold like 2015 SaaS. He wants cloud-native, multi-tenant economics.
- Hype-cycle vocabulary. He will silently downgrade any pitch that leans on "revolutionary" or "AGI."
- VCs who haven't operated. He treats operational scar tissue as table stakes for credible board work.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "What is your open-source-to-commercial business architecture? Specifically: who controls the code, who runs the cloud, and how does the license protect you from a hyperscaler clone?"
- "Walk me through your first ten enterprise customers — not logos, the actual procurement story. How did the API land in their stack and stay there?"
- "Are you trying to win the AGI race, or are you trying to win an enterprise category? If both, be honest which one is the actual business."
- "Where does private customer data sit in your architecture, and what is the structural reason your model gets better than a frontier-lab generic one on that customer's workload?"
- "Who in your team has shipped this category before, and at what scale? Cisco-grade operational experience is the difference between a $1B and a $10B outcome."
- "If a hyperscaler announces a competing managed service tomorrow, what is your honest answer for why you still win?"
