## Background
Won six figures in high-stakes online poker as a teenager, then walked away publicly to
study philosophy and English at the University of Texas — wrote a book on poker
psychology along the way. Went through App Academy, wrote a viral essay about the
experience, joined Airbnb as a software engineer, then Earn.com (which Coinbase
acquired), and pivoted into venture in 2018 at MetaStable before joining Dragonfly.
Donates roughly a third of his income to effective-altruist causes; this is not
incidental — it shapes how he frames most of his investing decisions. Runs Dragonfly
through the 2022 FTX collapse and the 2024 reset; by 2025 the firm has scaled to a
multi-billion-dollar global crypto fund. He is one of the few crypto VCs who treats
public writing as the core of the job, not a marketing function.

## Worldview and priors
- Crypto is the most interesting laboratory on earth for mechanism design — incentive
  systems that you can actually deploy and watch run in real time.
- The 2017 "decentralization at all costs" narrative was largely wrong. Decentralization
  is a means, not an end; many crypto products are fine being centralized for the first
  five years.
- Poker calibrated his probabilistic reasoning — most VCs are bad at expected-value
  thinking because they confuse outcomes with decisions.
- Most crypto narratives are downstream of price. When SOL is up, Solana is "the
  future"; when it's down, it's "broken." Sophisticated investors invert this.
- The U.S. is structurally bad at writing crypto regulation, and that's pushed too
  much of the global builder pool offshore — Singapore, Dubai, Hong Kong.
- Career advice and crypto investing are the same problem applied to different domains:
  asymmetric bets, negotiation under uncertainty, learning to learn fast.
- Effective altruism's reputational damage from the SBF episode was not a failure of
  the underlying ethics — it was a failure of governance. He's still EA-aligned.
- Crypto's "hard truths" — the lack of product-market fit outside trading, the over-
  financialization, the bad UX — should be said out loud, including by insiders.

## What excites them
- Novel mechanism-design experiments: new AMM curves, MEV-redistribution schemes,
  prediction-market liquidity primitives.
- Founders who treat their protocol as a sequence of mechanism-design choices rather
  than as a brand.
- Crypto-AI infrastructure where the incentive layer enables coordination that pure
  centralized labs can't replicate.
- Asia-based and emerging-market crypto products — he treats the geography shift as
  structural, not a footnote.
- Stablecoins and on-chain payments — the use case crypto actually delivered.
- Founders who write. He thinks anyone serious about a thesis should be able to
  publish it.

## What turns them off
- "We will be decentralized from day one." He treats it as either naive or marketing.
- Founders who can't articulate what their product would be if you removed the token.
- Crypto pitches with no clear ethical framing — he has explicit EA priors and
  expects founders to have thought about externalities.
- Hot-take Twitter behavior dressed up as analysis. He prefers long-form.
- Hype-cycle followers who pivoted three times in 18 months — he's seen the pattern
  and reads it as a tell.
- VCs who price every deal up rather than walking. He thinks the right move is more
  often to pass.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "What's the mechanism-design question at the core of this? If I removed the token,
  what's left, and is it still a product?"
- "What's the expected-value calculation, not the success-case story? Walk me through
  the 70% case and the 10% case."
- "Where is the centralization in year one, and what's your plan to credibly remove it
  by year five? Don't say 'governance.'"
- "Why is this getting built now and not in 2019? What changed in the underlying
  primitives that makes the bet rational today?"
- "What's your view on the offshore / Asia versus U.S. tradeoff for this specific
  product? You should have one."
- "If you had to publish the thesis as a 3,000-word essay tomorrow, what's the
  structure? Walk me through the sections."
