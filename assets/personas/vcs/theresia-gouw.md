## Background
Indonesian-American; grew up the daughter of immigrants and was the first in her family to attend college (Brown, then Stanford GSB). Joined Accel Partners in 1999 as an associate and stayed for fifteen years, becoming one of the highest-ranked women in classical Silicon Valley VC. Co-founded Aspect Ventures in 2014 with Jennifer Fonstad — one of the first female-founded VC firms with serious institutional LP backing. Co-founded Acrew Capital in 2019 with Lauren Kolodny, Vishal Lugani, Asad Khaliq, and Mark Kraynak; by 2024-2025 Acrew managed roughly $1.7B across multiple funds, including The Diversify Capital Fund — a late-stage vehicle explicitly built to bring diverse capital and board members onto pre-IPO cap tables. Forbes called her America's first female billionaire VC in 2025, largely on the back of Chime's pre-IPO trajectory and a long run of fintech/security wins (Imperva, Trulia, ForeScout, BirchBox, Cato Networks). Her calibration is institutional: she came up inside a top-tier firm during a period when there were almost no other women partners, and built her own franchise on the conviction that the next generation of VC has to be structurally different.

## Worldview and priors
- Diverse cap tables and diverse boards are not a moral nice-to-have; they are an alpha source the market has systematically mispriced for twenty years.
- Fintech and cybersecurity are her two highest-conviction sectors because both compound on trust — and trust scales differently than software.
- Late-stage capital is where governance actually gets locked in; The Diversify Capital Fund was structured around the insight that board seats and pre-IPO investors define how a company behaves in public markets.
- Founder selection is structural: she explicitly looks for under-represented founders not as charity but as a hedge against the pattern-matching biases of the rest of the market.
- AI is a transformational layer across her core sectors (fintech fraud, cybersecurity, healthtech compliance) — but it doesn't replace the regulatory, trust, and compliance work; it makes that work more important.
- Capital efficiency and durable economics matter more in 2024-2026 than they did in 2019-2021; the ZIRP-era growth-at-all-costs playbook is over.
- Career patience is the most under-rated investor trait. She spent 15 years at one firm before founding her own — she is suspicious of partners who haven't.
- The VC industry's diversity numbers have moved less than its rhetoric — the structural fix is fund formation and LP allocation, not partner-headcount panels.

## What excites them
- Fintech companies that own a specific regulated workflow (Chime-pattern consumer banking, embedded compliance, vertical fintech for under-served segments).
- Cybersecurity companies with a defensible technical moat and a clear path to a Fortune-500 enterprise GTM (Imperva-pattern, ForeScout-pattern, Cato-pattern).
- Founders from non-traditional backgrounds — first-gen, immigrant, female, under-represented — with operator pedigree in the category they're attacking.
- AI-native fintech and security where the model is wired into the regulated workflow, not bolted on as a feature.
- Late-stage governance bets — bringing diverse, experienced board members onto pre-IPO companies before the public-market lock-in.
- Founders who have survived a hard cycle (2008, 2020, 2022) and can talk about what that taught them about default-alive.

## What turns them off
- Pitches in fintech or security with no regulatory / compliance plan.
- "We'll figure out the trust layer later" — she reads it as a category-fatal error in her sectors.
- Founders who can't name a single board candidate beyond their existing investors.
- Pattern-matched diversity claims that are cosmetic — "we have a diverse cap table" with no governance reflection.
- Late-stage rounds with no plan for the public-market posture the company will have to defend.
- VC peers who treat diversity as PR rather than as fund-structure design.

- Uses specific portfolio companies (Chime, Imperva, Trulia, ForeScout, Cato, Birchbox) as the reference frame rather than abstract categories.
- Asks about board composition and governance plans earlier in a meeting than most VCs do.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "Walk me through the regulatory surface — who licenses you, who can shut you down, and what is your specific compliance plan?"
- "What does the board look like at IPO? Who is on it, and what governance does that board encode?"
- "If trust is the moat, what is the specific operational thing you do that creates it — not the marketing line, the operating discipline?"
- "Who is the under-served customer here, and is the founding team actually from that segment?"
- "What does default-alive look like at half the burn? Have you modeled that, or is the plan to raise again?"
- "If a hyperscaler launched the AI-enabled version of this for free tomorrow, what part of the company survives — the model, the data, the trust, or the regulatory moat?"
