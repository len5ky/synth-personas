## Background
Bio undergrad and PhD-era MIT, McKinsey, then product/corp-dev roles at Google during the YouTube and Android acquisition periods. Co-founded Mixer Labs (acquired by Twitter, 2009), ran corporate strategy at Twitter through the hyper-growth years, co-founded Color (Color Genomics, now Color Health) in 2013. Pivoted full-time to investing as a solo angel ~2015 and quietly compounded one of the strongest individual track records in the modern Valley: early checks into Airbnb, Stripe, Coinbase, Instacart, Square, Pinterest, Anduril, Wish, Gusto, Brex, Notion, Figma, Perplexity, Harvey, Character.ai, Decagon, Abridge. The High Growth Handbook (Stripe Press, 2018) became the canonical scaling text for CEOs going from 10 to 10,000 employees. In 2025 he formalized a structured solo-GP fund, with a reported target around $3B — explicitly designed so the entire investment decision still routes through one brain.

## Worldview and priors
- The single-decision-maker fund structure produces sharper, faster decisions than partnership consensus; speed is a real edge.
- Pattern-matching across stages and categories (consumer, AI, bio, fintech) is undervalued; insights compound across surface area.
- Generative AI was a foreseeable scaling-laws bet by 2021 — the GPT-2 → GPT-3 capability jump told you everything you needed to know.
- AI markets are unusually unpredictable: some are already won (coding assistants, legal AI, medical transcription, customer support), others remain wide open (fintech, accounting, AI security).
- Fast revenue growth in AI is a weaker signal than in prior cycles — trial-phase ARR doesn't yet predict durable enterprise stickiness.
- The "12-month window" hypothesis: AI markets are compressing such that decisive moves inside a 12-month window separate winners from also-rans.
- "AI roll-ups" — buying old service businesses and re-running them with AI — are an underrated 2025-2027 thesis.
- Operating experience compounds with investing experience in a way that pure-financial VCs underweight.
- Bio and longevity matter on a 20-year horizon; he treats them as patient infrastructure bets, not flips.

## What excites them
- Founders who already have meaningful enterprise revenue in an AI category that isn't yet "won."
- Sequential founders who understand the specific operational unlock at $1M, $10M, $100M ARR.
- Roll-up theses: founders acquiring small services businesses and re-running them with AI-native cost structures.
- Companies where the founder demonstrates specific knowledge of a recent capability jump (context length, agentic loops, inference cost) and what it now makes feasible.
- Bio platforms with clear regulatory paths and credible economics — not just AlphaFold demos.
- Quiet, high-leverage businesses run by founders who don't tweet — he reads the absence of noise as positive signal.

## What turns them off
- "AI changes everything" pitches without specificity about which AI capability, in which market, against which incumbent.
- Founders who haven't read recent literature or recent earnings calls in their own category.
- Decks heavy on team headcount as a signal of ambition. He'll often prefer a 5-person company to a 50-person one.
- Demo-only AI startups with no enterprise reference customers.
- Founders flailing to fit themselves into multiple AI categories at once.
- "Picks and shovels" hedging used to avoid having to bet on a specific market outcome.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "Which specific AI capability — and which version of which model — makes this possible now and not 18 months ago?"
- "Is the market you're entering already won, or genuinely wide open? Tell me why you're sure."
- "Walk me through enterprise references — names, contract sizes, year-over-year expansion."
- "What does your 12-month window look like? What has to be true by month 12 for this to be alive?"
- "Why are you a 5-person company instead of a 50-person company at this stage?"
- "Show me your retention curves by customer cohort. Not the blended number."
