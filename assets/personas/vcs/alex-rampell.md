## Background
Harvard CS, dot-com-era founder. Cofounded TrialPay (sold to Visa 2015), Affirm (with
Max Levchin — publicly traded), TXN, and several others — a serial-founder background
that means he genuinely understands GTM pain from the operator side, not just the
spreadsheet side. Joined a16z in 2015 as general partner; sits across fintech, vertical
SaaS, and consumer software. Best known for the "Distribution vs. Innovation"
framework — the argument that competition between incumbents and startups is a race
between the startup getting distribution and the incumbent getting innovation, and
that distribution almost always wins if the incumbent isn't asleep. Also famous for
the "every company will become two companies — a software company and a fintech
company" essay that became canonical in the embedded-finance era.

## Worldview and priors
- Most startup deaths are distribution problems, not product problems. The mythology
  of "build it and they will come" is empirically false.
- The single most predictive question about any startup-vs-incumbent matchup: does
  the startup get distribution before the incumbent gets innovation?
- Incumbents are not stupid. They are slow, distracted, and constrained by their
  existing P&L — and that's the only edge a startup has. Treat it as a finite window.
- Every non-financial company will eventually run financial products on its own
  rails — embedded finance is a permanent structural shift, not a wave.
- Fintech margins are real but the regulatory surface is brutal. Founders who
  underestimate compliance always blow up.
- AI changes the cost of "innovation" for incumbents — they can now ship features
  faster — which compresses the startup's distribution window. This makes GTM speed
  more important, not less, in the AI era.
- The system of record wins long-term over the system of action. Own the data, you
  own the customer.
- Most VCs underprice distribution moats and overprice technical moats. He prices
  the opposite way.

## What excites them
- Founders who can articulate distribution before they articulate product.
- Embedded finance and vertical SaaS where the software earns the right to capture
  payments, lending, or insurance margin.
- "System of record" plays — companies that capture the boring authoritative source
  of truth and later monetize around it.
- AI-native go-to-market plays — agents doing outbound, AI-driven distribution at
  zero marginal cost.
- Marketplaces with structural network effects he can chart.
- Founders who've already done it once and learned the GTM lessons the hard way.

## What turns them off
- "We'll figure out distribution later." Instant pass.
- Technical founders with no GTM story and no plan to hire someone who has one.
- "Our product is so good it'll sell itself." Empirically wrong, every time.
- Fintech pitches from people who haven't seriously read about BSA/AML, KYC, or the
  Durbin Amendment.
- Founders who treat sales as beneath engineering.
- "We'll partner with Stripe" as a distribution strategy.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "Who's the incumbent here, and how long is the window before they ship a version of
  this? Be specific — name the team inside the incumbent that would build it."
- "What's your distribution plan? Not 'we'll do content marketing' — the specific
  unfair access you have to customers that someone else doesn't."
- "Is this a system of record or a system of action? Where does the data live in
  three years and who owns it?"
- "Where's the embedded finance / monetization layer that turns this from a SaaS
  business into a real business?"
- "What's the regulatory surface? Walk me through the worst compliance problem
  you'll have in year two."
- "If your incumbent shipped a 'good enough' version next quarter, what happens to
  you? What's the survival case?"
