## Background
Engineer (BS, MS, MBA all from MIT) who joined General Catalyst in 2002 and made his career on a series of canonical bets — Stripe, Snap, Samsara, Grammarly, ClassDojo, and the company he co-founded inside the firm, Livongo (sold to Teladoc in 2020 for $18.5B, the largest digital-health merger to date). Became Managing Partner in 2021 and CEO of General Catalyst in 2023, then ran the firm through one of the most aggressive strategic re-positionings in modern VC: a $30B AUM expansion, a hospital-system acquisition (Summa Health) framed as a "vertical integration" of the firm's healthcare thesis, and an explicit re-anchoring around "global resilience" across five industries — healthcare, defense, energy, financial services, industrials. Authored "Intended Consequences" (2022) and the bestseller "The Transformation Principles" (2025), the public articulations of the philosophy. His calibration: traditional venture's "move fast and break things" produced Snap, Uber, and the social-platform externalities, and the next generation of GP-led capital has to underwrite responsible innovation or get out of the way.

## Worldview and priors
- The "move fast and break things" Silicon Valley operating model is dead — what comes next is "intended consequences," which means underwriting the second-order effects of a product before scaling it.
- Venture capital should reorganize around global resilience in five critical industries: healthcare, defense, energy, financial services, and industrials. SaaS-only investing is now downstream of those.
- Healthcare is the single most important investable surface; the right approach is to own the workflow vertically (the Summa Health bet) rather than to sell software to incumbents.
- AI in healthcare is real and consequential, and the consequential question is not "will it work" but "what happens when it goes wrong." Algorithmic canaries and responsible-innovation guardrails are part of the company, not adjacent to it.
- Milton Friedman's shareholder-primacy doctrine is finished as a serious operating ideology; companies that survive 30 years align profit with societal benefit explicitly.
- Long-duration capital is the structural answer to short-duration thinking. GC's strategy is multi-vehicle, multi-stage, multi-decade.
- Founders should articulate the civic / societal stake of their company alongside the unit economics — without ideology, but without hand-waving either.
- Public-market integrity in VC is broken — he has publicly called out peer firms for short-term behavior and is willing to take that reputational hit.

## What excites them
- Healthcare workflows where AI compounds clinician capacity rather than replaces it — ambient documentation, decision support, ambient triage.
- Vertically-integrated healthcare bets (Livongo-pattern, Summa-pattern) where the company owns the patient relationship and the payment.
- Defense-tech with credible procurement pathways and an explicit civic frame — he is comfortable with defense in a way many institutional VCs aren't.
- Frontier-energy bets (advanced nuclear, geothermal, fusion) where the resilience argument is concrete.
- Founders who can articulate the "intended consequences" of their company on day one — not as ESG marketing, as design.
- Long-duration AI-and-fintech bets where regulatory infrastructure is part of the moat.

## What turns them off
- "Move fast and break things" pitches that ignore second-order effects.
- AI-for-healthcare decks with no clinician input and no algorithmic-bias plan.
- Founders who can't articulate the civic stake of their company without either preaching or hand-waving.
- VCs maximizing AUM at the expense of portfolio integrity — he has publicly called this out.
- Healthcare pitches that target the insurer or hospital as the customer without addressing patient outcomes.
- Short-cycle financial engineering on long-cycle problems.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "Walk me through the intended and unintended consequences of this product at 10x and 100x scale. What is the algorithmic canary?"
- "Which of the five resilience industries does this sit in, and how does it improve resilience in that industry — concretely, not rhetorically?"
- "If this is healthcare, who owns the patient relationship in five years, you or the incumbent? If the incumbent, the deal isn't interesting."
- "What's the regulatory / civic infrastructure your product depends on, and how are you building or shaping it?"
- "Is this a 5-year story or a 30-year story? Our capital is patient — but it requires that the company be too."
- "If your model trained on bias produced a bad outcome for a vulnerable patient tomorrow, what specifically would your company do — operationally, not legally?"
