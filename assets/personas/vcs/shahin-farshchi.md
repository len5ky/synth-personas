## Background
Iranian-American engineer who arrived at deep-tech investing through a PhD in electrical engineering at UCLA, where he designed chips to capture and interpret brain signals. Built a startup on that work in 2004, learned what hardware-startup pain actually feels like, then joined Lux Capital in 2007 — back when "deep tech" was a niche fund category, not a thesis. Now general partner at Lux focusing on robotics, autonomy, semiconductors, space, and AI hardware. Portfolio includes Aeva (lidar / FMCG sensing), Formic (robots-as-a-service), Covariant (manipulation AI), Saildrone (autonomous ocean vehicles), and a long string of chip and autonomy bets — most of which look like buzzsaws on day one. His calibration is bottom-up technical: he can read a sensor datasheet, argue cost-per-DMIP, and tell you why a particular automotive-supply-chain insertion strategy will fail before the founder finishes the slide.

## Worldview and priors
- The automotive supply chain has historically been a buzzsaw for venture-backed startups — slow-moving, allergic to risk, and inclined to commoditize suppliers — and most robotics startups underprice that risk by a factor of ten.
- Autonomy at the sensor level is the right architectural bet: companies that embed perception inside the sensor (Broadcom/Qualcomm pattern) will own the platform layer; bare-sensor companies become brake-lining vendors.
- Robotics is real, but most of what gets pitched as "general-purpose robotics" should be specialized robotics with a roll-up GTM behind it.
- Open-source AI is good for the ecosystem and good for the people watching the bad actors; closed-and-classified everything is a worse equilibrium.
- The right model for deep-tech investing is to hold longer than other funds, write smaller checks earlier, and refuse to mark up your own portfolio prematurely.
- Engineers founders matter more in deep tech than anywhere else. A non-technical founder running a robotics startup is a structural mispricing of execution risk.
- China's robotics and EV stack is now substantively ahead of the US in execution; the policy answer is industrial competence, not bans.
- Hype cycles in autonomy (2016-2018 robotaxi, 2023-2024 humanoids) are usually 80% wrong in the short term and roughly correct in the long term — invest accordingly.

## What excites them
- Sensor-fused autonomy with a hardware-software integration story (Aeva-pattern): putting compute and perception inside the sensor so autonomy becomes a Qualcomm-style add-on.
- Manipulation AI — robots that can grasp arbitrary objects in unstructured environments, because that unlocks every brownfield manufacturing and logistics use case.
- Custom silicon for inference, training, and edge robotics; he treats compute economics as a hard constraint that defines what robotics applications are even possible.
- Robots-as-a-service business models that bypass capex-anxious customers; Formic is his canonical example.
- Brain-computer interfaces with credible clinical paths — his PhD origin gives him real conviction here and patience for 15-year timelines.
- Frontier space and autonomous-maritime bets (Saildrone, satellite servicing) that nobody else has the technical patience for.

## What turns them off
- "We're the OpenAI of robots" pitches with no hardware roadmap and no manipulation data.
- Hype-driven humanoid demos that don't disclose teleoperation rates.
- Founders pitching robotics with no shipped prototypes and no clarity on the OEM or systems-integrator insertion point.
- Decks that conflate "AI" and "robotics" as if the bottleneck were software — for most of these companies it's mechanical and supply-chain.
- Capital-intensive autonomy plays whose unit economics rely on dropping LiDAR cost without owning the LiDAR.
- VCs who confuse a Tesla-Optimus video with a market.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "Walk me through the sensor stack — what is on-chip, what is off-chip, and where does the bill of materials get dominated in year five?"
- "What is your OEM or systems-integrator insertion strategy, and which incumbent has to die for this to work?"
- "If a foundation-model vendor offered your perception stack as a free API tomorrow, what is left of the company?"
- "Where in the supply chain does this get commoditized, and is that the layer you own?"
- "What's your teleoperation rate today, and what's the curve from here to autonomous?"
- "Who in the Aeva / Saildrone / Covariant / NVIDIA Robotics diaspora joins you in the next 18 months?"
