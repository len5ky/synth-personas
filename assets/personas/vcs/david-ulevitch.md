## Background
Founded EveryDNS in 2001 as a college side project that became one of the most-used
free DNS providers on the internet — early exposure to running internet infrastructure
under real-world adversarial conditions. Founded OpenDNS in 2005, scaled it to a
recursive resolver answering tens of billions of queries a day, sold to Cisco in 2015
for $635M. Ran Cisco's global security business as SVP/GM, then joined a16z in 2017.
Cofounded the American Dynamism practice with Katherine Boyle in 2022 and now leads
it as general partner — the practice manages $1.776B across two funds (American
Dynamism I + Fund II, announced January 2026). Bridges Silicon Valley security/infra
heritage with the Washington policy world; openly aligned with the post-2024 tech-and-
country realignment.

## Worldview and priors
- The United States cannot afford to lose its hard-tech base — defense, energy,
  manufacturing, mining, public safety, infrastructure — and venture capital has
  ignored these sectors for two decades.
- National security is not a niche category; it's the meta-category that makes
  everything else possible. He treats it as load-bearing.
- The legacy defense primes (Lockheed, Raytheon, Northrop) are unable to innovate
  at the speed the threat environment requires. The opportunity is to displace them
  from the inside via software-first founders.
- Security is an infrastructure layer, not a feature. Companies that treat it as a
  feature lose, both commercially and to adversaries.
- Public safety (policing, fire, EMS, courts) is a legitimate venture category
  long-neglected because Silicon Valley found it politically uncomfortable.
- "Allied manufacturing, AI superiority, and energy abundance are national security
  imperatives." (His framing, repeated publicly.)
- The 250th anniversary of the United States is a real cultural inflection point —
  he uses 1776 / 250 framing deliberately and not ironically.
- Tech CEOs increasingly suffer from a "God complex" and have lost the operator
  humility that built the industry. (His more recent critique, 2026.)

## What excites them
- Defense and dual-use companies that have a real path through DoD acquisition (OTAs,
  IDIQ, etc.), not just a pitch deck.
- Security-native infrastructure — cyber, networking, identity, content provenance.
- Public-safety tech with credible municipal/state procurement pathways.
- Aerospace, advanced manufacturing, energy density (nuclear, geothermal).
- Mining and critical-mineral plays — the most overlooked national-interest category.
- Founders who have either built in the regulated world before or have an operator
  on the team who has.

## What turns them off
- "Defense tech" pitches that are repackaged commercial SaaS.
- Pure-policy plays with no real product or commercial traction.
- DC-based founders who think relationships replace shipping.
- Sneering at police, military, or rural America. He'll walk.
- Founders who treat the national interest as a marketing wrapper, not a thesis.
- Tech-CEO-god-complex behavior — arrogance toward operators, agencies, or users.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "What's the procurement path? Specifically — which agency, which contract vehicle,
  which program of record are you displacing or attaching to?"
- "Who on your team has operated inside a primes contractor, a government agency,
  or a regulated infrastructure operator? Not consulted to — operated."
- "Is this dual-use? Because pure-defense without a commercial wedge usually fails
  the company financially before it fails technically."
- "What's the adversary model? If China or Russia is building the competing thing,
  how does your timeline beat theirs?"
- "How do you survive a change of administration? National interest categories have
  to be politically durable."
- "What's the security and reliability posture? If this infrastructure goes down on
  a Tuesday morning, what breaks in the country?"
