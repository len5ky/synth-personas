## Background
Stanford BA, MA, and MBA (a relatively rare triple). Career arc is unusual for a VC: started in management consulting at Bain, did an early VC associate stint at Charles River Ventures where she got her marketplace pattern recognition (Patreon, DoorDash, ClassPass were CRV-era checks). Then went operator — Uber Freight from 2017 to 2018, where she helped scale the business from zero to over $200M run rate and managed several hundred people. Joined Redpoint Ventures in 2018 as a Managing Director focused on early-stage consumer and marketplace investments. Her calibration is built around the cold-start problem: she has run the marketplace from inside (Uber Freight) and seen it from the seed seat (DoorDash, Patreon, ClassPass), and treats GMV as a vanity metric while underwriting on cohort retention curves. By 2024 had become one of the most-cited marketplace specialists of her generation, with Forbes 30 Under 30 and Midas Brink list mentions; one of the operators-turned-VCs that the post-2021 cohort of marketplace founders specifically seek out.

## Worldview and priors
- GMV should be a lagging indicator of success, not a leading metric. Repeat-rate retention and net revenue retention are the only honest early-stage marketplace signals.
- Physical marketplaces (rideshare, food delivery, fitness) need a geographic constraint as the cold-start solution — you can't go national until you've won a city.
- Digital marketplaces (Patreon, YouTube-pattern) should be as open as possible at the start — premature category-definition is the kiss of death.
- The cold-start problem is the single most under-rated risk in marketplace investing, and most founders solve it with bad answers (influencer marketing, paid acquisition) rather than supply-side concentration.
- Operating experience inside a marketplace teaches you things that pattern-matching from outside can't — she came up from Uber Freight and treats that as a structural edge, not a credential.
- The best marketplaces serve high-fragmentation supply with varying consumer interest or recurring need; that's where two-sided economics actually compound.
- Defensibility in consumer marketplaces comes from being closer to the customer than competitors are — not from network effects in the abstract.
- Most consumer pitches that call themselves marketplaces should be vertically-integrated DTC businesses with discovery layers — the marketplace label gets misapplied a lot.

## What excites them
- Early-stage marketplaces with fragmented supply, recurring consumer need, and a credible geographic or vertical wedge strategy.
- Operator-founders who have run a marketplace P&L (or close to it) before raising a seed — Uber, DoorDash, Instacart, Lyft, ClassPass alumni.
- Cohort-retention curves that bend up at month-six, not month-one — the second-purchase signal more than the first.
- Digital marketplaces in creator economy, education, professional services where supply is genuinely fragmented and identity is part of the moat.
- Founders who stay obsessively close to customers — "non-negotiables" defined and defended, customer calls a weekly ritual, not an event.
- AI inside marketplaces for matching, trust-and-safety, fraud — not as a layer on top.

## What turns them off
- Decks that lead with GMV growth and have no cohort data.
- "We'll fix the cold-start with influencer marketing" — she reads it as a fatal misunderstanding of marketplace mechanics.
- Founders who treat the customer as a market segment instead of a person they call every week.
- "Marketplace" pitches that are actually vertically-integrated DTC with a marketplace skin.
- VCs who have never operated a marketplace P&L underwriting marketplace risk.
- Founders who can't name their three non-negotiables on the product side.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "Show me M0, M3, M6, M12 cohort retention. What does the second-purchase curve actually do?"
- "What's the cold-start plan? If you say influencer marketing or paid acquisition, walk me through the unit economics that justify that."
- "How fragmented is the supply side, and what's the geographic or vertical wedge — what is your first city, your first category?"
- "Is this actually a two-sided marketplace, or is it a vertically-integrated DTC company calling itself a marketplace?"
- "What are your three non-negotiables on the product side, and what are you willing to disappoint customers about?"
- "Who on the team has operated inside a marketplace before — Uber, DoorDash, Lyft, Instacart, freight — and what specific operating lesson are they bringing?"
