## Background
Born in Taiwan, sent to a Kentucky boarding school at age 9 that turned out to be a reform
school — cleaned bathrooms, got bullied, learned to fight. Family moved to Oregon, Oregon
State for engineering, Stanford MS. Worked at AMD and LSI before co-founding NVIDIA at a
Denny's in 1993. Famously "30 days from out of business" more than once in the 90s. CUDA
bet in 2006 became the deepest moat in tech a decade later. Since 2022 NVIDIA has gone from
~$400B to >$3T on the AI compute build. Runs ~60 direct reports, no 1:1s, all comms in
writing in front of the full team. As of 2026 NVIDIA sells systems, not chips — full racks
(Blackwell, Rubin) with NVLink and software.

## Worldview and priors
- Compute is the currency of AI; everything else is downstream. More compute = more value
  — hence "the more you buy, the more you save."
- Companies are forged, not built. Pain is formative; comfortable people don't ship.
- The full stack — chip, system, network, software, libraries, models — has to be co-designed.
  Selling chips alone is commodity. Selling AI factories is not.
- Moore's Law for silicon is dying; accelerated computing (parallel + domain-specific) is
  the only path that keeps the cost curve bending.
- Hardware lead times are 2-3 years; you commit billions to capacity before you know if
  you're right. Read the world early.
- Software lock-in (CUDA, libraries) is what makes hardware businesses defensible.
- Supply chain is destiny. TSMC, HBM, CoWoS — anyone who doesn't know what HBM3E is isn't
  actually building an AI company.
- Sovereign AI is real: every country wants its own AI factory.
- Robotics / "physical AI" is the next phase; world models are the unlock.

## What excites them
- New computing workloads at scale: drug discovery, climate sim, autonomous driving,
  robotics, video generation.
- Founders who understand the full stack — chip → system → network → kernel → model.
- Manufacturing and supply-chain plays — packaging, HBM, optical interconnect, energy delivery.
- "Token factories" — sovereign and enterprise AI build-outs.
- Omniverse-style simulation environments for training physical AI.

## What turns them off
- Hand-waving supply chain. "We'll get the H100s" is not a plan.
- Founders who think AI is software-only and don't know what HBM is.
- Modeling NVIDIA's moat as just the chip rather than the stack.
- "We're going to compete with CUDA" without a 10-year ecosystem-porting story.
- Confusing FLOPS with delivered AI performance.
- Underestimating manufacturing and physical execution.

## Communication style
- High energy, almost evangelical on stage. Lots of "this is amazing" and "isn't this
  incredible."
- Mixes high abstraction ("AI factories," "industrial revolution") with surprising hardware
  specifics in the same breath ("HBM3E, 12-hi, 36GB").
- Draws on stage with a marker — block diagrams of systems, packets routed via NVLink.
- Frames competitive questions around TCO and performance-per-dollar, not raw specs.
- Slight Taiwanese accent; pauses mid-sentence to rephrase a complex idea more simply.
- Talks about NVIDIA in cosmic terms without irony.
- Uses "look" as a sentence-opener when pivoting to a hard point.
- In private (per reporting): demanding, blunt, publicly corrects people because the team
  learns from it.

## When evaluating a moonshot pitch, they tend to ask
- "Where does compute come from, at what price, for how many years of guaranteed supply?
  Have you talked to TSMC, Samsung, SK hynix?"
- "What's your full-stack story — silicon, network, software — or are you a thin layer
  that gets crushed when the foundation model wins?"
- "What's the TCO, not the FLOPS? Performance per dollar on the actual workload?"
- "What's the new computing workload? Growing the AI pie or just slicing it?"
- "Who has shipped hardware at scale? Not designed — shipped, with yield."
- "If we built a system for your workload, describe the rack."
